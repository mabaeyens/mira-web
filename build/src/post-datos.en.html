My data is public!

Apr 2026 · First published on LinkedIn, in Spanish

There is a phrase I hear a lot: "my data cannot leave here".

I get it, and it is almost never true.

Your email is in Exchange Online or Google Workspace. Your contracts are signed by everyone in DocuSign. Your sales team lives in Salesforce. Your whole office works in Microsoft 365.

All of that is another company's servers, in some datacenter you cannot see, run by people you do not know.

And yet: "my data cannot be there!". The same data that is already spread across half a dozen different places.

I am not saying security is a minor topic. It is serious, with real regulation and real consequences. Europe's GDPR does not forgive, and there are multimillion fines to prove it. What I am saying is that "cloud" does not mean "insecure", just as "on-prem" does not mean "secure".

An on-prem rack has a purchase price and a list of costs nobody adds up in full: power, cooling, licences, upgrades, the team that maintains it.

The cloud has its risks too, and they are real: unoptimised queries, virtual machines nobody switches off, data pipelines that process more than they need to, storage that grows with nobody governing it, AI models that consume more resources than planned. It has happened to all of us.

And then there is the human factor, which underlines the real problem. In the end, someone exports to Excel, emails it, and goodbye data governance. That is not an infrastructure problem, it is a culture problem we will talk about another time.

Netflix decided to go to the cloud in 2008 after an outage that left them without service for three days. The migration took them seven years, but they multiplied their user base by eight in that period. Without that bet, the business as we know it today would not have been possible.

37signals, the creators of Basecamp, went the opposite way: they were spending 3.2 million dollars a year on AWS, bought their own servers for 600,000 dollars, and cut their infrastructure bill by between 50 and 66%. For their kind of business, stable growth and predictable loads, the cloud was simply expensive.

Both made the right decision. Cases like Netflix's are plentiful; cases like Basecamp's are rare, and notorious for that reason.

By now the question is no longer whether the cloud is secure. It is whether on-prem is as secure as you think. And whether the decision about where your data lives is yours, or someone made it years ago and nobody has reviewed it since.
