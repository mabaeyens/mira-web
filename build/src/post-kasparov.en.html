AI is not new. It is just new for everyone at once

Mar 2026 · First published on LinkedIn, in Spanish

In May 1997, Deep Blue beat Kasparov. A fascinating event. I cut out the news story and pasted it on the cover of McGraw-Hill's C++ Reference Manual, which I was studying at the time. I do not remember whether it was from a newspaper or a magazine. It was not a long article, but I thought it deserved to be there, visible.

To many of my friends the event looked like a fluke. Science fiction.

To me it looked inevitable.

My argument was simple: chess is a limited game. It has millions of possible moves, but they are finite, and all follow the same rules. A computer is very good at doing lots of calculations very fast.

I had spent years watching each new generation of processors beat the last one, memory grow, storage get cheaper. If that progression did not stop, it was a matter of time before a computer always won. Not sometimes, always. At any game.

They looked at me in surprise, and their answer was almost always the same: "but human intuition, imagination, instinct. A machine cannot copy that."

They were right about that. Deep Blue had no intuition. It calculated 200 million positions per second. It did not understand chess. It did not improvise. It also had no nerves and no mother in the room watching it play. Kasparov did, and he lost. Brute force was enough.

Almost thirty years later, I work with artificial intelligence every day. And I am surprised how little the underlying conversation has changed.

Back then the question was: could a machine always win? Today it is: can AI help you be more competitive, faster, better informed? Can you use it to your advantage?

The answer looks like the old one. It depends. It depends on the problem, on what you ask of it, and above all on whether you understand what the machine can really do for you.

LLMs, the language models behind ChatGPT and the like, do not reason like we do. But connect them to your tools and the capabilities multiply in a way we had not seen before. The tool does not need to replace you to be useful. Maybe one day it will, but today what matters is that you know how to use it.

I lost the book in some move, and the clipping with it.

Today it is news that a language model has a one-megabyte window. One megabyte. A 3.5-inch floppy disk.

There is a long way to go. And that is fascinating.
